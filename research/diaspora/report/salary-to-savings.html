Salary to Savings

What happens to an Indian professional's pay between the payslip abroad and the money arriving at home, whether what survives the journey still beats inflation in India — and what the Indian career you left behind was doing meanwhile.

It measures salaried migrants throughout. That is a real limit and not a quibble — for someone who owns the business abroad, none of the arithmetic below holds. The last section explains why, and why no published figure can tell the two apart.

A salary earned abroad passes through a series of claims before any of it is useful in India. Each stage consumes what the last one left, so the order matters and the losses compound. This report walks that chain for the seven countries where it can be measured on comparable terms, then asks three things the chain cannot answer on its own: against what you paid and gave up, was it worth it — what is the surplus already promised to — and does any of it hold once rent starts moving?

Everything is in PPP-adjusted US dollars. That is not a cosmetic choice — it is what makes the cost-of-living stage tractable at all, and it is explained at stage 3.

STAGE 1

Earnings

OECD average annual wages, 2024, USD at purchasing-power parity.

The spread is wider than the tax debate that usually dominates these conversations. The United States pays 68% more than Japan for the average job. Before a single deduction, the country you work in has already decided most of the outcome.

These are national averages, and that matters later: Indian migrants in the US and UK earn above their host average — the population concentrates in technology, medicine and finance — while Indian migrants in the Gulf earn far below theirs. Only the Gulf half of that distortion has been corrected here, which makes every Gulf-versus-West comparison in this report the conservative one.

Two assumptions worth knowing

The US figure blends federal tax, FICA and a mid-range state. State choice moves it about ten points either way — Texas and Florida levy nothing, California around nine percent — and that is not modelled. Singapore's rate is income tax only, which is correct for this population: CPF does not apply to Employment Pass holders, though a citizen or permanent resident would face roughly 20%.

STAGE 3

Expenses

One real basket, US$28,000 a year, identical across every country.

This looks like a shortcut and is the opposite. The obvious construction — take nominal wages, subtract a cost-of-living index — double counts, because the OECD wage series is already deflated by each host country's price level. Cost of living is inside the wage number before you start.

Working in PPP dollars throughout dissolves the problem. In PPP terms a fixed real standard of living costs the same everywhere by construction, so the whole cost-of-living stage collapses into a single constant. The basket size is then the only free parameter, and the ranking survives every value tested between US$20,000 and US$36,000 — only the margins move.

The honest limit: this is one standard of living, not a personal budget. Someone sharing a flat and cooking at home clears a far smaller basket than the figure assumes; someone with children in private school clears a much larger one.

STAGE 5

The inflation hurdle

Money earned abroad is a claim on Indian goods, so the hurdle is set in India.

This is the stage the ranking above cannot see. Savings sent home are eroded at the Indian rate, not the host rate — and which Indian rate depends entirely on what the money is for. The bars show the nominal wage growth a remitter needs simply to stand still, against the low single digits that advanced-economy wages actually deliver.

Shaded band: typical advanced-economy nominal wage growth, 2–4%. Bars beyond it are not cleared.

The finding

Medical support and household bills at home are comfortably cleared. Education and general consumption are close to a coin toss. But property and gold — the two things remittances most often accumulate toward — are cleared by no advanced-economy wage at all. Someone saving abroad to buy Indian property is losing ground every year the transfer is deferred, however well their destination scores on tax.

STAGE 6

Return on the whole thing

Against what you paid to get there, and the Indian salary you stopped earning.

Everything above measures a level. Return needs two more terms: the cost of going, and the counterfactual — what the career you left would have done if you had stayed.

The cost. For Gulf labour migration it is measured: the KNOMAD/ILO survey of returning workers puts recruitment costs at one to nine months of foreign earnings — US$1,260 to US$11,342 at the Gulf blended wage, paid before the first day of work. For skilled migration to the West no comparable figure was reachable, so the table below runs at a US$15,000 assumption and is swept beside it.

Six of seven repay the entire cost of migrating inside a year. On this term the answer is trivially yes — which is exactly why this term alone is not the return.

The counterfactual. India's statistics ministry measures what a salaried Indian earns, and how fast it is rising: ₹19,316 a month in 2022, ₹20,408 in 2023, ₹21,455 in 2024. That is a compound growth rate of 5.39% a year.

Shaded band: nominal wage growth in the advanced economies through this cycle, 2–4%.

The crossover

The Indian salary you left is growing faster in nominal terms than the foreign salary you took — 5.39% against 2–4%. One side is measured and the other is a cycle range, so read it as a direction rather than a decimal; but the direction is not close. In real terms the same thing from the other end: an Indian salary gains roughly +0.94% a year in purchasing power, while savings sent home are eroded at 4.45% — or 8.30% if they are meant for property — having grown at only 2–4%.

So a foreign life buys a step change in level, not a faster rate. The step is large and it arrives quickly. But the rate underneath it is worse than the one you left, and the erosion applies at Indian prices no matter where the money was earned. Three things follow, and none of them are about tax:

  • The gain is front-loaded. It is banked in the early years, while the level gap is widest — not accumulated evenly across a career.
  • Deferring the transfer destroys it. Money held abroad to send later compounds at the host rate and is spent at the Indian one.
  • What the money is for decides whether it worked. Against consumption the arithmetic holds. Against property it does not — and property is what remittances most often accumulate toward.

One caveat cuts in the reader's favour. The Indian benchmark is the all-India salaried average, and people who migrate are not average — they skew graduate and urban. The salary actually given up is therefore higher than ₹21,455, which makes the foreign premium smaller than it looks here, not larger. The crossover is the conservative reading.

STAGE 7

What the surplus is already promised to

Debt service comes out of the same money that funds the remittance.

Every figure so far treats the surplus above the basket as free to send home. For a US-based Indian professional it usually is not: a degree was financed, a car was financed, a house may be. This section is United States only, and for a precise reason — the US is the base currency of the purchasing-power conversion used throughout, so its PPP dollars are simply dollars, and these payments subtract with no exchange-rate step. That holds for no other destination here.

The three debts are not the same kind of thing, and treating them alike is the mistake worth avoiding. A student loan is a pure claim — nothing comes back. A car loan is also pure cost, because the asset depreciates. A mortgage is neither. It replaces rent the basket already contains, so subtracting it would count housing twice; and its principal is not spending at all but saving, redirected into American property instead of Indian. On an early payment only $260 of $1,930 builds equity — which is when that redirection is least efficient. So no mortgage is netted below.

Where a remittance is not a remittance

An Indian who financed a US master's with an Indian education loan services a rupee debt out of dollar income — and those payments cross into India and are counted as remittances. They are not family support and they are not savings. They are loan repayment. At $9,593 to $14,780 a year per borrower, this is one concrete channel by which India's remittance figures measure something other than migrants sending wages home.

The sensitivity runs the opposite way to everything else here: a weaker rupee makes the loan cheaper to service out of dollars. It is the only line in this report where rupee depreciation helps the person who left.

One caution about the last row. It puts a debt-laden American against six peers carried as debt-free, and that is not a fair fight — people who move to Germany, Canada or Australia borrow too; their figures simply were not gathered. What is genuinely particular to the United States is that a master's there costs ₹70–100 lakh while German public universities charge no tuition at all, that car dependence is structural rather than chosen in most American metros, and that the Indian education loan exists at the scale it does precisely because US study is what it is most often taken out to fund.

STAGE 8

And rent does not stand still

Non-US destinations. The basket was held constant; rent is not.

Holding living costs at a fixed $28,000 is fine for comparing countries in one year and wrong for asking whether the surplus lasts. Rent is the largest and fastest-moving thing in that basket — and there is a catch in how it is measured that changes the answer.

Every country publishes two rent figures. The consumer price index measures the rent paid across the whole rented stock, most of which is occupied by people on running contracts, shielded by caps, indexation and long leases. Separately there is what someone signing a fresh lease actually pays. A migrant is always in the second group. They arrive; they sign at market.

So the published number — the one every cost-of-living comparison uses, including the basket in this report — understates what a migrant pays, and understates it most in exactly the countries whose tenant protections are strongest.

Bar: pay minus rent on new lettings — the migrant's number. Dot: the same on the published CPI basis. Right of centre is pay winning.

Three countries where the basis flips the sign

In Japan, Germany and the Netherlands, a migrant is gaining ground on rent according to the published series and losing it according to the one that applies to them. Japan is the starkest: CPI rents rose 0.7% while new listings in Tokyo's 23 wards rose 7.82%. Over ten years on the migrant's own basis, the Japanese surplus falls by 70% — a thin margin becoming almost nothing.

Canada runs the other way and is the one genuinely favourable case in the set. Asking rents have fallen for twenty-two consecutive months, so someone arriving now signs into a falling market while wages still rise.

VERDICT

The ranking, after everything

Three horizons, and the places where they agree.

These are salaried-worker rankings. Every figure in them comes from a wage, a tax schedule and a living-cost basket, which is a description of an employee's finances and of nobody else's. Read on for what that excludes.

There is no single weighted score here, and its absence is deliberate. Trading savings against residency against a rent trajectory would need conversion rates between things that do not convert, chosen by me — and the number that emerged would look more certain than any of the figures inside it. Three orderings shown honestly is the better answer.

The three: what lands in year one debt-free; the same after debt; and the surplus a decade out, on the rent a migrant actually signs. Across all three, nothing moves. That stability is itself the finding — the ordering survives a debt adjustment and ten years of divergent rent inflation. What changes is the size of the gaps, not who is ahead.

SCOPE

The other population

Everything above describes people on a payslip. It does not describe people who own the business.

The chain in this report takes a wage, applies a tax rate, subtracts a basket and calls what remains remittable. Each of those three steps quietly assumes an employee. Income is a wage, so it has a ceiling. Tax is set at source by a published schedule. And what can be sent home is bounded by what is left after living.

For someone running their own business abroad, none of the three is true.

Why no figure anywhere can separate them

Under India's foreign exchange rules, foreign income of every kind — a salary, a company's profits, savings built up over years — enters through the same NRE account and is freely repatriable on the same terms. A nurse in Dubai sending part of her pay and a trading company owner repatriating a year's profit use the same channel, the same account type and the same forms.

So the statistics record them identically. There is no field that distinguishes them. No amount of care with the published remittance numbers will separate the two populations, because the separation was never captured.

The tax difference is not simply that owners pay more, though on the face of it they do — American self-employment tax is 15.3% against an employee's 7.65%, because the employee's other half is paid by the employer. Above roughly $80,000 of income, though, an owner can split earnings between a salary and distributions and change what is exposed to that rate at all. For the employee the rate is imposed; for the owner it is chosen, within limits. That is the difference that matters, and it is invisible in any table of statutory rates.

The earnings run the same way. Indian-run businesses in the United States report average owner income of $84,080 against $52,086 for American business owners generally — and against the $82,933 average wage this report has used throughout.

This resolves something left open earlier

The Gulf figures never quite worked. Wage data anchored on Qatar's statutory minimum put the typical Indian migrant's pay at about $15,000, and the remittance estimate of $8,659 a head only reconciled with that if you assumed the top of every band at once — blue-collar workers on full packages and professionals at the very top of the scale.

Split the population and it resolves without strain. If a small minority own businesses, the rest can remit at ordinary rates and the average still lands where it does:

Bars: what each business owner must remit for the observed average to hold, assuming everyone else remits 40% of pay. Shaded to the dashed line: the blended Indian migrant wage in the Gulf, $15,123.

The mechanism, at last

At a 5% owner share — unremarkable for the Gulf — the implied figure is about four times the blended wage. Ordinary for someone running a trading or retail business. Impossible for an employee.

So the gap never needed heroic assumptions about how much of their pay migrants send home. It needed a modest minority moving sums that are unexceptional for business owners and simply unavailable to anyone on a salary. Every section of this report has circled the same conclusion — that India's remittance figures measure more than migrants sending wages home. This is not a flaw in the measurement. It is two populations sharing one channel.

LIMITS

What this does not show

The Gulf is absent, and it is the largest bloc. Its wage figure was corrected — recruitment-survey national averages overstated the Indian migrant wage by four to five times, and an ILO-anchored model built on Qatar's statutory minimum puts the blended figure at US$15,123. But no migrant-specific living cost exists for it. Applying this report's US$28,000 basket returns negative savings, which the observed remittance flow falsifies outright. Inferring the basket instead gives about US$539 a month — corroborated by Qatar's minimum wage being structured as pay plus accommodation plus food in kind — but that figure is derived from the remittance number, so using it would make Gulf savings equal Gulf remittances by construction.

Immigration status is a second axis, and it runs the other way. For Indian nationals the US employment green-card queue runs 12–15 years or more; as of the August 2026 Visa Bulletin the EB-2 India cut-off is unavailable because the annual cap is exhausted. The cause is the 7% per-country cap, which applies regardless of population. The same job converts to permanent residence in about a year in Canada or Australia. That wait attaches to nationality, not to skill or salary — a colleague born elsewhere in the identical role does not face it. It does not make the money ranking wrong; it makes it one axis of two, and for many people the slower axis decides.

Also unmodelled: currency risk, investment returns, healthcare and schooling quality, job availability in a given field, and whether you would want to live there. Wage levels are national averages rather than Indian-migrant-specific outside the Gulf, which understates the advanced economies.
